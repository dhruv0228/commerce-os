Black Friday, Christmas and the long term: one plan you can follow

Your eight questions answered, the words explained, and the plan laid out in three lanes: a small Black Friday lane to learn on, a Christmas lane, and the long-term Barkfare build. It also covers the "$50 million" reel and the Amboras tool. Nothing has been bought, sent, published or spent.

Your eight questions, answered short

1
Are there products online that rank higher than the first 36?
One beats them all: your own extender, if a cheaper listing checks out.

Scouts checked 23 more products and a skeptic re-checked the best ones. The big lesson: every high price we found belongs to a brand with thousands of reviews, and generic copies sell for a third to half of it. Copying a winner does not copy its price. The exception is your own extender: a US listing seen today at $54.01 landed would leave $30.43 an order at new-store ad cost. It is a lead to check, not a supplier yet.

2
Is there advertising that needs less money than Meta?
Yes, if you only pay when someone buys.

Affiliate creators take 10% to 27% of a sale, and only when it happens. Organic posts and Google free listings cost nothing. None of them is proven cheaper per customer for a new pet store, and none brings volume fast.

3
What are creator deals like, and are they cheaper?
Paying a small creator per post is usually more expensive per customer, not less.

A UGC video for your ads costs $89 to $259. A post on a small dog account runs about $50 to $225 (unverified). Such posts get about 350 to 2,429 views. To beat Meta, a $50 post would need about 7,966 views if 1 in 100 viewers clicked through. Commission deals are the exception.

4
What else can we do that is creative and still works?
Sell kits, collect emails, list on Google for free, and partner locally.

Bundle the extender with a hammock. Collect emails with a welcome offer. List free on Google Shopping. Ask dog trainers and groomers to share a link for a commission. Later, put the kit on Amazon, where ads are much cheaper per buyer.

5
Would some products sell better through creators?
Yes: products under about $50 with a result you see in 3 seconds.

Creator sales cluster at low prices: pet items sold through TikTok Shop videos average $36.94. The hammock (muddy dog, clean seat) and the knife sharpener (paper slice) fit. A $149 extender sells better through ads, with creators filming the video.

6
What is the plan for Black Friday, Christmas and the long term?
Learn small now, test the extender for Christmas, build Barkfare in January.

Black Friday: the hammock under the Barkfare name, with organic posts, 3 gifted creators and capped ads, about $347 in total. Christmas: the extender if the cheaper listing checks out. January: the 5-kit own-brand batch, Amazon and a weekly product scanner.

7
What do the words mean?
Part 1 has a plain-words glossary.

Each word has an everyday explanation and an example worked out on your extender. Read the picture of one sale first. It shows where every dollar goes.

8
What exactly do I do, step by step?
Part 6 has ten steps, starting today.

How this business works, in plain words

You run a store. A supplier holds the stock and ships each order to your customer. Your job is to get buyers to the store and make sure each sale leaves money after every cost. Here is how the money moves, using the Barkfare extender as the example.

The most you can pay to win a buyer without losing money. It equals what is left before ads.

75% of break-even. The house rule keeps a 25% safety cushion for surprises.

Extender: $38.81.

Cushion rule

Only go ahead if the benchmark ad cost is at or under the target ad cost. It stops thin products from eating your budget.

Go price

The highest supplier cost at which the cushion rule still passes. If the real cost comes in above it, do not run ads.

Extender: the supplier cost must be $83.13 or less.

LTVlifetime value

What one customer leaves you over time after costs, including reorders. For a one-time product it is just the first order.

LTV to CAC

Lifetime value divided by ad cost per customer. The reel's rule says 3. Our house rule asks for 1.33 on the first order.

Running it
Dropshipping

The supplier ships each order straight to your customer. You never touch stock.

US stock

The supplier ships from a US warehouse: 4 to 8 business days. China-shipped takes 2 to 4 weeks and causes refunds.

Float

Cash you put up front: you pay the supplier and Meta on the day of the sale, Shopify pays you 7 to 14 days later.

Extender: about $135 per order until payout.

Payout

When Shopify sends the customer's money to your bank. New stores wait longer.

Blind shipping

No supplier name, invoice or price inside the box, so the customer sees only your brand.

3PL

A warehouse you pay to store your own stock and ship each order. Used once you buy stock in bulk.

Stop rulekill rule

A rule set before you start: pause an ad that has spent its break-even ad cost with no sale.

Gate

A check that must pass before any money moves. Example: the confirmed supplier cost is at or under the go price.

Chargeback

A buyer disputes the charge with their bank. You lose the sale, the product and a fee of about $15. Slow delivery is a common cause.

Creators and content
UGCuser-generated content

A short video a creator films with your product, which you then run as your ad. You pay per video.

Paid post

You pay a creator a flat fee to post about your product on their own account.

Giftingseeding

You send free product to creators and hope they post. No fee, no guarantee.

Affiliate

The creator gets a share of each sale they bring, for example 15%. You pay only when someone buys.

Spark or partnership ad

You pay Meta or TikTok to show a creator's own post as an ad, with their name on it.

Organic

Free posts on your own accounts. No cash, but it takes time and most posts get few views.

Each row is what it costs to win one customer for the dog hammock ($69), on a scale from $1 to $10,000. The dashed line is its break-even ad cost: what is left before ads, $34.96. A bar to the left of the line makes money. A bar to the right loses it.

What this means

Two things are true at once. Paying per sale (affiliate) is the only way to make every order profitable on a cheap product: the hammock keeps $16 to $28 an order. But affiliates bring as many sales as creators choose to post, which may be none. Paying per post on small accounts is a gamble that usually loses. A $50 post needs about 7,966 views to match Meta, and typical small posts get 350 to 2,429. So creators are best used two ways: on commission, and to film the videos you run as ads. The skeptic agreed: no source shows creator or partnership ads are cheaper per purchase for a new pet store.

Products creators sell well

  • Under about $50, where creator sales cluster (pet items on TikTok Shop average $36.94).
  • A result you can see in 3 seconds: muddy dog then clean seat, or a paper slice after sharpening.
  • The creator already has the user on camera: their dog, their car, their desk.
  • Giftable in December. Toy video ads returned 2.90 in December against 1.46 in July.

Products creators sell badly

  • Expensive items like a $149 kit. Gifting one to a creator who never posts loses the whole kit, and fewer than half of gifted creators post.
  • Pet products in general on video ads: pet finished last of 14 categories in one 80,000-ad study.
  • Products you do not own on TikTok Shop. US sellers must own the stock and ship in 2 business days.
  • Anything where the creator would have to claim results they have not seen. The FTC rule says they must have tried it.

Part 7

The "$50 million" reel and the Amboras tool

Short version: the story is not supported, the reel is an ad, the 3-to-1 rule is real but often misused, and Amboras is a real but very young tool with no independent proof behind its claims.

What holds up and what does not

  • The "$50 million in 26 days" story: no news story, buyer announcement or public profile backs it. No YouTube channel called "Luca Maratti" and no such video turned up on YouTube, Google or the Wayback Machine. Copies of the story on other small accounts change the details: age 18 or 22, $48.3M to $50M, 22 or 26 days.
  • It is an ad, in practice: several copies carry #amboras or #amboraspartner, the script matches other Amboras creator posts, and job posts offer creators scripts and pay. This reel shows no paid-partnership label in its caption, which does not prove it was unpaid.
  • Its proof: a screen recording of the Amboras app on a demo store, claiming 2.7% conversion against 1.1% on Shopify, with no sample size or dates. The same numbers appear in other creators' reels.
  • What is real: the offer ideas it points to. AG1, IM8 and Grüns all use subscriptions, first-order discounts and welcome kits. IM8's Black Friday page offers a free $123 welcome kit with a 90-day plan. These are supplement brands (outside your rules), but bundles, gifts with purchase and add-ons carry over to Barkfare.
  • Grüns is real money: Unilever announced it is buying Grüns (April 2026, price not disclosed), after a reported $300M yearly run rate. That is a venture-funded brand, not a playbook a $1,800 store can copy.

The "LTV must be 3 times CAC" rule, tested on your options

The rule comes from software investing (David Skok), who calls it only a guideline. Careful sources say the "value" must be profit per customer, not sales. Real consumer brands often sit below 3: one large Shopify dataset has a median of 2.4, and 52% of brands lose money on the first order. Here is how your options score, with value measured strictly as money left before ads.

What the rule means for you

Only the extender on Amazon reaches 3, because Amazon buyers cost about $13.10 each, not because they come back. A one-time $149 kit will not have the reorders that make supplement brands work. What moves your number is the cost of the product and the size of the order: the cheaper extender listing scores 2.08 and the $189 bundle on bulk stock 2.19. Use the house rule (1.33 on the first order), not the reel's number, as the gate for a first test.
