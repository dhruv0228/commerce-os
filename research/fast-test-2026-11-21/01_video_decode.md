## 0. One-paragraph answer

The creator is Austin Rabin (YouTube, 217K subscribers). The video is a 38-minute setup tutorial that never launches an ad, never takes an order and shows no result from the demo store. The "$4.2M" belongs to Haven Golf Company, a golf polo brand that holds its own inventory in a North Carolina warehouse and runs Meta ads at what he calls "around a two ROAS". By his own words in another video it is "not drop shipping". Every tool in the tutorial pays him or a partner: a Shopify referral, a Zendrop partnership, an ad-spy tool he says "we created", and a $97/month coaching Discord. The method itself (competitor ad research, AI image ads, store copy cleanup) is ordinary and usable, but it is a creative workflow, not evidence that a Zendrop dropship store makes money.

### What could get the store or the ad account shut down

1. AI images that differ from the real item. He replaces the supplier's real photo with a generated "premium" hero image and judges it by looks ("looks way better actually"). If the delivered product does not match, expect "not as described" disputes. He states that Shop campaigns require a dispute rate under 1% (nmBkTOUTsHY, min 03, his statement; Shopify's own threshold not checked). Meta's ad standards prohibit deceptive commercial practices and exist "to protect users and businesses from being deceived out of their money" (VERIFIED overview: https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/fraud-scams/unacceptable-business-practices/ ; detailed rules not retrieved).
2. Copying a competitor's ad look. The reference brand, oneisall, is a real pet grooming company selling its own dryer and vacuum kits at $97.99 to $179.99 (VERIFIED: oneisall.com/products.json). Ads built to look "so much like their ads" for a competing dryer invite an intellectual property complaint and takedowns. Our rule already says: borrow an angle or a format, never the asset.
3. Hiding AI use from Meta. In the September video he advises generating without text and finishing in Canva so "you don't get flagged for AI on Facebook", and exporting through Canva or Photoshop "because it's going to erase that metadata information and now Facebook's not going to be able to identify that it's AI generated content." Meta says it labels "ad images created or edited using third-party AI tools with AI info" (VERIFIED: https://www.meta.com/en-gb/help/artificial-intelligence/355108217670024/ , updated four days before this research). Whether stripping metadata is itself a violation, and the penalty: UNKNOWN. It conflicts with our rule to label AI creative where platforms require. Do not copy this step.
4. Shipping-time claims. Any delivery promise on the page or in an ad must be one Zendrop can meet for that exact product. The seller, not the supplier, is responsible (FTC, above).
5. Prices or claims baked into images. The generated ad pulled the store price into the image. A later price change makes the ad wrong.
6. "Always allow" permissions on connectors and a never-expiring Zendrop API token with write scopes. One bad instruction or a compromised tool can change products or place orders. Keep writes on approval and tokens short-lived.
7. A new store, new ad account and new payment account all started the same day, sending paid traffic at a generic template that many other stores share. That profile is the one most likely to see payout holds or ad review. (Our reading; no primary figure.)
8. Unbacked income claims if Dhruv ever markets like this. The FTC's 2024 "Operation AI Comply" cases targeted sellers of "AI-powered" store schemes over earnings claims (ESTIMATED: FTC press release of September 2024 via search summary; not read in full today). This is about the people selling the dream, not about a store owner, but it shows how regulators view the pitch.
